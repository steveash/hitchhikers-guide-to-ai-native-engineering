# Our approach to government and national security partnerships

> OpenAI publishes a formal "National Security Principles" document governing
> government and law-enforcement use of its technology — naming four categories
> of use it will not support (mass domestic surveillance, unaccountable
> high-stakes decisions, unaccountable use of force, evasion of legal oversight),
> disclosing nine named allied-government cyber-defense partnerships under a
> program called "Daybreak," and explicitly prioritizing the United States and
> its allies for national-security AI access on explicit geopolitical grounds.

## Source Context

- **Type**: blog-post (official `openai.com/index/` company announcement,
  "Global Affairs" category, July 8, 2026, unsigned/institutional byline) plus
  a linked 5-page PDF, "OpenAI Principles for National Security Partnerships"
  (`cdn.openai.com/pdf/openai-principles-for-national-security-partnerships.pdf`),
  which is the substantive document — the blog post itself is a short
  announcement/summary that link out to the principles PDF.
- **Author credibility**: First-party institutional statement from OpenAI, the
  company most central to this corpus's frontier-lab coverage. The principles
  document states it was produced through "a cross-company effort," facilitated
  by "a leading national security expert, David Kris" (former Assistant
  Attorney General for National Security, though the post itself does not give
  his title — only names him), with "listening sessions across the company"
  and input from research, safety, policy, and government-partnerships teams.
  This is the highest-authority tier of source (official published company
  policy, not a leaked memo or secondhand report) for *what OpenAI's stated
  policy is* — but, as with `blog-openai-built-to-benefit-everyone.md`, stated
  policy is not independently verified adherence. Unlike that prior OpenAI
  mission post (rated `anecdotal` for containing zero checkable specifics),
  this document names specific, falsifiable facts: nine named allied
  governments, a named program (Daybreak), a named model (GPT-Rosalind), and
  specific contractual restrictions already in force under an existing
  Department of War agreement — which is why this note rates `emerging`
  rather than `anecdotal` overall.
- **Scope**: Covers OpenAI's stated principles for government/national-security/
  law-enforcement use of its technology, four categories of prohibited use, its
  eligibility framework for government partners, its stated allied cyber-defense
  and biodefense partnerships to date, and its position that some AI-in-government
  questions should be resolved by legislation rather than by OpenAI itself. Does
  NOT cover: any technical enforcement mechanism for the stated restrictions
  (no description of how "no autonomous weapons" is technically verified or
  audited), any named agency deployment beyond the high-level "Department of War"
  reference, independent verification of the nine cyber partnerships' actual
  scope or activity, or how disputes/violations of the principles would be
  identified or remediated.

## Extracted Claims

### Claim 1: OpenAI is publishing formal "National Security Principles" developed through a cross-company process, facilitated by an outside national security expert, to govern its government and national-security partnerships going forward, including its existing Department of War work
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the announcement post.
- **Confidence**: settled (first-party statement of what was published and how)
- **Quote**: "We engaged a leading national security expert, David Kris, to help facilitate the process and provide his independent judgment, held listening sessions across the company, and engaged employees representing a range of teams from research and safety to policy and government partnerships."
- **Our assessment**: The internal-consultation process is itself a notable governance artifact — a frontier lab describing a formal, cross-functional (research, safety, policy, government-partnerships) internal process, mediated by a named external expert, for producing a public policy document. It signals that these principles are meant to bind future OpenAI decisions on national-security deals, not just describe current practice.

### Claim 2: OpenAI states it will not support use of its technology for mass domestic surveillance, unaccountable high-stakes decisions (including use-of-force decisions), or uses that evade legal obligations or oversight
- **Evidence**: The "National Security and Law Enforcement Uses OpenAI Will Not Support" section of the principles PDF lists these as named, bulleted restrictions.
- **Confidence**: settled (explicit first-party policy statement)
- **Quote**: "we do not support the use of OpenAI technology for the following: Mass domestic surveillance... High-stakes decisions without appropriate human judgment and accountability... Use of force without appropriate human judgment and accountability... Evading legal obligations, oversight, or accountability."
- **Our assessment**: This is the single most concrete, checkable commitment in the document — a named, bulleted prohibition list rather than abstract mission language. It gives practitioners and policy teams something falsifiable to point to if a future OpenAI government deal appears to cross one of these lines. It is still a self-policed commitment with no named external audit or enforcement mechanism described anywhere in the document.

### Claim 3: OpenAI states these principles are consistent with, and formalize, contractual restrictions already in force under its existing agreement with the U.S. Department of War: no use for mass domestic surveillance, no use to direct autonomous weapons systems, and no use for high-stakes automated decisions
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the announcement post connecting the new principles to a prior, already-signed agreement.
- **Confidence**: settled (first-party statement that this is a pre-existing, already-contracted restriction, not a new commitment)
- **Quote**: "When we announced our agreement earlier this year, we articulated several contractual restrictions: no use of OpenAI technology for mass domestic surveillance, no use of OpenAI technology to direct autonomous weapons systems, and no use of OpenAI technology for high-stakes automated decisions. Those restrictions are consistent with the principles we are sharing today."
- **Our assessment**: This is a retroactive confirmation that OpenAI already has a live Department of War contract carrying these three restrictions, dated to "earlier this year" (i.e., before July 2026) — meaning OpenAI has been supplying technology directly to a national military department for months prior to this principles publication. The principles document formalizes and generalizes restrictions that were previously scoped to one specific contract.

### Claim 4: In the past month, OpenAI's "Daybreak" cyber defense program has established "Trusted Access for Cyber" partnerships with nine named allied governments/institutions: Australia, Canada, Japan, Republic of Korea, France, Germany, Poland, the Netherlands, and EU institutions such as ENISA — plus a separate, growing UK government partnership on cyber testing and evaluation
- **Evidence**: Direct enumeration in the announcement post.
- **Confidence**: settled (first-party, specific, dated, named-partner claim)
- **Quote**: "In the past month, as part of our Daybreak cyber defense program, we have already established Trusted Access for Cyber partnerships with Australia, Canada, Japan, Republic of Korea, France, Germany, Poland, the Netherlands and EU institutions like ENISA. We also have a growing and trusted partnership with the UK government around cyber, testing and evaluation."
- **Our assessment**: This is the most concrete, novel factual claim in the source — a named program with a specific, countable list of allied partners established within a one-month window immediately before publication. It is independently checkable in principle (partner governments could confirm or deny), though this note has not independently verified it with any of the nine named governments or ENISA.

### Claim 5: OpenAI announced expanded trusted access to its GPT-Rosalind model for select U.S. government and allied partners supporting public health and biodefense missions
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the announcement post.
- **Confidence**: settled (first-party, specific claim; references a "last month" announcement not itself fetched for this note)
- **Quote**: "We are taking a similar approach in biosecurity. Last month, we also announced expanded trusted access to our GPT‑Rosalind model for select U.S. government and allied partners supporting public health and biodefense missions."
- **Our assessment**: A second named model (GPT-Rosalind, not previously documented anywhere in this corpus) tied to a specific use domain (biodefense/public health) and a specific access tier (select U.S. and allied government partners, not general availability). Combined with Claim 4, this establishes that OpenAI now has at least two named, government-restricted-access model/program tracks (Daybreak for cyber, GPT-Rosalind for biodefense) distinct from its consumer/enterprise ChatGPT and API products.

### Claim 6: OpenAI explicitly acknowledges that government use of AI can concentrate state power and reduce human judgment in consequential decisions, and states that its national security principles exist specifically to prevent that outcome
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the principles PDF's introduction.
- **Confidence**: settled (explicit stated rationale for the entire document)
- **Quote**: "But they can also concentrate state power, reduce human judgment in consequential decisions, and amplify the harm caused by government mistakes. The core question, then, is who gets to decide how governments use AI, for what purposes, and subject to what limits and accountability."
- **Our assessment**: This is a direct, explicit acknowledgment — from OpenAI itself, in a national-security-specific context — of the same power-concentration risk that `blog-openai-built-to-benefit-everyone.md` Claim 7 asserted OpenAI's mission counteracts only in the abstract ("we believe the safer future is one where power is broadly distributed"). Here the risk is named specifically rather than only gestured at, and the document proposes concrete restrictions (Claim 2) as the mitigation — a more operationalized statement than the earlier mission post, though see Cross-References for the tension this still creates with Claim 7 below.

### Claim 7: OpenAI states the United States has a "distinctive role" as its "home jurisdiction," gives explicit geopolitical reasoning for prioritizing the U.S. and its allies for national-security AI access, and states it expects to prioritize work with "key U.S. allies and partners" beyond the U.S. itself
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the principles PDF, Principle 2 ("We prioritize partners who share our values").
- **Confidence**: settled (explicit stated policy, not an inference)
- **Quote**: "But if the U.S. and its allies are denied access to advanced AI while authoritarian states develop or obtain it, the global balance of power could shift against democratic governance, human rights, and the rule of law. We therefore expect to work with the United States and its allies on national security uses of AI... We also expect to prioritize work with key U.S. allies and partners whose access to AI can help preserve a global balance favorable to democratic governance."
- **Our assessment**: This is the claim most directly relevant to the filed contradiction in issue #1597 (see Cross-References) — a first-party OpenAI document explicitly describing a tiered, nation-aligned access model for national-security AI, justified on geopolitical grounds, from the same company that months earlier published "give everyone on Earth a personal AGI" as a headline mission goal. The document does try to distinguish the two (this is scoped explicitly to "national security and law enforcement uses," not general consumer/API access), but the reasoning given — access as a lever in a "global balance of power" contest between democratic and authoritarian states — is the same nation-state-alignment logic Armin Ronacher's essay describes as embedded in US AI safety discourse generally (see `blog-ronacher-ai-nationalism-americans-only.md` Claim 2).

### Claim 8: Alliance or partner status alone is not sufficient for eligibility — OpenAI states it will also weigh democratic governance, human rights protections, oversight strength, and the specific risk of the proposed deployment, and may narrow, suspend, or end partnerships over "sustained or systemic misalignment"
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the principles PDF, Principle 2.
- **Confidence**: settled (explicit stated policy)
- **Quote**: "Some important U.S. allies or partners may not fit neatly within a democracy-based framework. In those cases, strategic alignment may matter, but it should not decide the question by itself. We will also be guided by democratic governance, protection of individual rights, respect for human rights, meaningful oversight, the strength of applicable legal and accountability frameworks, and the specific risks of the proposed deployment... Sustained or systemic misalignment with a partner may lead us to narrow, suspend, or end a partnership, or to reassess eligibility."
- **Our assessment**: This qualifies Claim 7 — the eligibility framework is not purely alliance-based, and OpenAI explicitly flags that some U.S. allies may not clear its bar. No named criteria for what counts as "sustained or systemic misalignment," nor any named instance of a partnership actually being narrowed or ended, are given — so this remains an untested policy commitment rather than a demonstrated practice.

### Claim 9: OpenAI states that meaningful human judgment over high-consequence AI-assisted decisions must be "meaningful in practice, not merely formal," and names specific failure modes — reviewer deference to system outputs, insufficient bandwidth to assess outputs, and inability to understand or intervene in system behavior — that can make nominal human oversight non-functional
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the principles PDF, Principle 4.
- **Confidence**: emerging (a specific, named risk-analysis framework, though not tied to any measured incident or audit in this document)
- **Quote**: "Human judgment over critical decisions must be meaningful in practice, not merely formal. As AI systems become more capable and more deeply embedded in operational workflows, human reviewers may defer to system outputs, lack the bandwidth to assess them, or be unable to understand and intervene in system behavior."
- **Our assessment**: This is the most specific and portable risk-analysis idea in the document — it names the exact mechanism ("automation bias" / rubber-stamp review) by which a "human in the loop" requirement can become non-functional, rather than simply asserting that a human must be present. This is directly useful as a general agentic-AI governance concept beyond the national-security context: any org relying on "a human reviews the output" as a safety control should check for these same three failure modes.

### Claim 10: OpenAI states it does not consider itself the appropriate body to oversee government use of AI, and instead frames its role as helping build technical capacity for external democratic oversight institutions (regulators, auditors, courts, lawmakers) — while separately stating it supports legislative efforts to establish safeguards on the highest-risk military AI uses
- **Evidence**: Two related direct statements, one in the principles PDF (Principle 3) and one in the announcement post.
- **Confidence**: settled (explicit stated position)
- **Quote**: "OpenAI is not the right institution to oversee government use of AI. But we can help regulators, auditors, courts, lawmakers, and oversight bodies build the understanding and practical capacity to oversee AI use at speed and scale, including through the use of AI itself."
- **Additional quote** (from the announcement post): "That is why we support legislative efforts to establish safeguards around some of the highest-risk military uses of AI, including in the contexts of domestic surveillance, autonomous weapons systems, and other high-risk uses that directly affect U.S. national security."
- **Our assessment**: OpenAI explicitly declines self-appointed oversight authority and calls for external, legislative safeguards on its own highest-risk use categories — a notable instance of a frontier lab publicly requesting binding regulation of itself rather than only self-regulation. No specific legislation is named or endorsed, so this remains an abstract policy position rather than a concrete regulatory commitment.

### Claim 11: Targeting-related military AI uses are not categorically disallowed by the principles, but systems that automate substantial parts of the targeting process or perform functions bearing directly on use-of-force decisions require "heightened scrutiny" based on their proximity to the use of force and whether humans can effectively manage their operation
- **Evidence**: Direct statement in the principles PDF's "Use of force without appropriate human judgment and accountability" bullet.
- **Confidence**: emerging (a specific, nameable policy distinction, though the criteria for "heightened scrutiny" and who applies it are not further specified)
- **Quote**: "Targeting-related uses are therefore not categorically disallowed, but because targeting is one important subset of force-related decision-making, systems that automate substantial parts of the targeting process or perform critical targeting functions that bear directly on decisions about the use of force require heightened scrutiny."
- **Our assessment**: This is a deliberately non-absolute line — OpenAI is not committing to "OpenAI technology will never be used in targeting systems," only that such use requires unspecified additional review. Readers should not interpret Claim 2's "no autonomous weapons" framing (as paraphrased in press coverage) as a blanket prohibition on any targeting-adjacent AI use; the actual document draws a narrower, more permissive line than a plain reading of "we don't support autonomous weapons" would suggest.
