# New policy ideas for the Intelligence Age

> OpenAI announces $1M in cash grants plus up to $1M in API credits split
> across 14 independent research/policy organizations in the US, EU,
> Brazil, Singapore, and South Korea, following through on an April 2026
> "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age" commitment — nine projects
> on broadening economic opportunity (workforce disruption scenarios, AI
> dividend distribution, "Right to AI" ownership models, state-level data
> center energy policy, person-based benefits, AI taxation, national
> economic-policy working groups, and two Brazil-based research/health
> projects) and five on societal resilience (an operational framework for
> measuring and coordinating response to recursively self-improving AI, a
> cross-border AI-bio risk information-sharing architecture, national-
> security-professional AI-safety training, privacy-preserving policy
> simulation, and AI-assisted legislative-oversight quality assessment in
> South Korea).

## Extracted Claims

### Claim 1: OpenAI is awarding a total of $1 million in cash funding plus up to $1 million in API credits across 14 grants to independent organizations, selected from more than 400 applicants who responded to an open call for proposals
- **Evidence**: Direct statement of the program's scale and selection process.
- **Confidence**: settled (specific, first-party, falsifiable figures)
- **Quote**: "More than 400 people and organizations responded to our call for proposals. Applicants identified an idea and proposed projects that could meaningfully contribute to public understanding and policy development. OpenAI will provide a total of $1 million in funding and up to $1 million in API credits across the selected projects."
- **Our assessment**: This is the single most concrete, falsifiable commitment in the post — a specific dollar figure and applicant count. Notably modest in absolute scale: $1M cash divided across 14 organizations averages roughly $70K per grantee before API-credit value, a small figure relative to OpenAI's overall scale and relative to the six-month research scope some grantees describe (e.g., commissioned reports, workshops, prototypes). Should be cited as evidence of a real, funded, checkable commitment, but not overstated as a major financial investment in independent AI-policy research.

### Claim 2: The grant program explicitly follows through on a commitment OpenAI made in an April 2026 document, "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age," to support outside researchers "testing, challenging, and building on" its own policy ideas
- **Evidence**: Direct statement connecting this announcement to the earlier document.
- **Confidence**: settled (first-party statement of intent and provenance)
- **Quote**: "The grants follow through on a commitment we made when we published Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age in April 2026: to support others in testing, challenging, and building on the ideas we put forward to ensure that advanced AI benefits everyone."
- **Our assessment**: This is a real instance of a frontier lab inviting outside challenge to its own policy proposals, which is a more concrete governance posture than pure mission rhetoric. It only partially delivers on "challenging," though: OpenAI selected the 400+ applicants and controls which 14 got funded, so the program does not test whether OpenAI would fund research that concludes its own "Industrial Policy" framework is wrong — none of the 14 project summaries in this post are framed as challenging OpenAI's positions, only as extending or operationalizing adjacent questions. The April 2026 "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age" document itself was not fetched for this note and is not yet in the corpus (see Guide Impact).

### Claim 3: OpenAI states that policy choices about how AI's benefits are distributed "should not be made by technology companies alone" and should instead "be shaped through democratic institutions and public debate," with independent research organizations playing a role that includes "challenge[ing] assumptions—including ours"
- **Evidence**: Direct statement of governance philosophy framing the grant program.
- **Confidence**: emerging (an explicit, specific governance position — more concrete than a pure mission slogan — but self-reported and not tested against a case where OpenAI actually accepted being challenged)
- **Quote**: "These choices should not be made by technology companies alone. In democratic societies, they should be shaped through democratic institutions and public debate. Independent research and policy organizations play an especially important role: they can develop and test new approaches, challenge assumptions—including ours—and serve as laboratories for ideas that governments can adapt to their own communities and circumstances."
- **Our assessment**: This directly echoes the self-abnegation-of-authority stance OpenAI took in its national-security principles document ("OpenAI is not the right institution to oversee government use of AI" — see Cross-References). Here the same posture is applied to economic and social policy rather than security policy: OpenAI states it should not be the sole decision-maker, while remaining the funder that selects which independent voices get resourced to participate in the debate — a structural tension the post does not address.

### Claim 4: OpenAI restates its position from "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age" that governments "should be prepared to use the full policy toolbox—from research funding and workforce development to market-shaping tools and targeted regulation—to help societies navigate a technological transition of this scale," while stating "no company or government has all the answers"
- **Evidence**: Direct restatement in the closing section, "What comes next."
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (a policy-advocacy position restated from an unfetched source document, not itself new evidence)
- **Quote**: "In Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age, we argued that governments should be prepared to use the full policy toolbox—from research funding and workforce development to market-shaping tools and targeted regulation—to help societies navigate a technological transition of this scale. But no company or government has all the answers."
- **Our assessment**: This is advocacy language restating an unread source document (the April 2026 paper), not new content from this post itself. It is directionally notable — an explicit call for "targeted regulation," not just workforce development — but the guide should not cite this post as the primary source for OpenAI's regulatory position; the April "Industrial Policy for the Intelligence Age" document itself would need to be mined separately for that.

### Claim 5: One funded project (Institute for Security and Technology) will build a measurement and coordination framework for "recursively self-improving (RSI) AI systems," including technically grounded definitions of uncontrolled self-modification, an incident-classification schema, and escalation pathways for cross-lab and industry-government coordination
- **Evidence**: Named grantee project summary.
- **Confidence**: emerging (a specific, named, funded project with a defined six-month scope — more concrete than most of the post's other project summaries — but no output exists yet to evaluate)
- **Quote**: "IST will establish a measurement and coordination framework for recursively self-improving (RSI) AI systems. This project develops technically grounded definitions, observable indicators of uncontrolled self-modification, incident taxonomies for cross-lab communication, and escalation criteria that map technical signals to coordinated response decisions. Outputs include an incident-classification schema, escalation pathways for industry-government coordination, and a public executive summary."
- **Our assessment**: This is the most directly relevant claim in the source to existing corpus RSI coverage — it is a concrete, funded (if modest) instance of the "build tools to pace/coordinate on RSI" ask that 1,171 frontier-lab employees, including OpenAI's own, put to governments in the July 2026 "Pace" letter (see Cross-References). It shows at least one frontier lab moving from signing an open letter to funding a named third-party organization to build the coordination infrastructure that letter called for — though the deliverable is still six months out and unaudited.

### Claim 6: A second resilience-track project (Nuclear Threat Initiative's biosecurity program, "NTI | bio") will test the legal feasibility of secure international information-sharing on AI capabilities, risks, and mitigations specific to AI-bio risk, then design a basic information-sharing architecture
- **Evidence**: Named grantee project summary.
- **Confidence**: emerging (specific, named, funded project; no output yet)
- **Quote**: "NTI | bio will test the legal feasibility for secure international sharing of AI capabilities, risks, and mitigations. NTI | bio will then design a basic information-sharing architecture to enhance international AIxBio safety and security."
- **Our assessment**: Extends the biodefense thread already in the corpus from OpenAI's national-security principles post (GPT-Rosalind's restricted biodefense access — see Cross-References) from a product-access-control approach to a cross-institutional information-sharing-architecture approach. The two together suggest OpenAI is pursuing AI-bio risk mitigation on at least two separate tracks (restricting its own model's access, and funding external legal/architecture work on international information sharing), though this project is pre-legal-analysis and has produced nothing checkable yet.

### Claim 7: A funded project (Yonsei University) will test whether "transparent, human-validated AI methods" can assess the quality of legislative oversight in South Korea's National Assembly, producing an analytical report, pilot dataset, and codebook on evidence use, polarization, and oversight quality
- **Evidence**: Named grantee project summary.
- **Confidence**: emerging (specific, named, funded project; methodology described at a high level only)
- **Quote**: "Yonsei University will test whether transparent, human-validated AI methods can help assess the quality of oversight in South Korea's National Assembly. The initial phase will produce an analytical report, pilot dataset, and codebook examining evidence use, polarization, and the quality of legislative oversight."
- **Our assessment**: A concrete instance of "AI used to evaluate the functioning of democratic institutions" rather than just "AI used inside industry or government workflows" — a use case not otherwise present in this corpus's governance coverage. "Human-validated" is asserted but not described mechanically (what validates what, at what sampling rate, against what ground truth are all unspecified), so this should be read as a research proposal, not a demonstrated audit method.

### Claim 8: The economic-opportunity track includes a project (European Centre for International Political Economy) to develop a practical framework for a "Right to AI," comparing employee ownership, citizen investment, pension-based ownership, social wealth funds, and intellectual-property participation as models for broadening access to AI-driven productive assets
- **Evidence**: Named grantee project summary.
- **Confidence**: emerging (specific, named, funded project with a defined comparative scope; no output yet)
- **Quote**: "ECIPE will examine how broader access to AI and ownership of productive assets could help people participate economically as technology becomes more capable. The project will develop a practical framework for a 'Right to AI' and compare models including employee ownership, citizen investment, pension-based ownership, social wealth funds, and intellectual-property participation."
- **Our assessment**: This is the most direct attempt in the source to operationalize the "broadly distributed power/benefits" language OpenAI has used in prior mission posts (see Cross-References — Extends) into a concrete comparative policy framework, rather than leaving "broadly shared benefits" as pure rhetoric. It is one funded research proposal among many competing models, not evidence that any of the five named ownership models will actually be adopted anywhere.

### Claim 9: The projects run for six months, with results due in 2027, spanning the United States, European Union, Brazil, Singapore, and South Korea
- **Evidence**: Direct statement of program timeline and geographic scope.
- **Confidence**: settled (specific, first-party, falsifiable scheduling claim)
- **Quote**: "Projects will run for six months, with results reported in 2027." / "The projects span the United States, the European Union, Brazil, Singapore, and South Korea and address two broad questions: how AI can broaden economic opportunity, and how societies can build resilience as capabilities advance."
- **Our assessment**: Sets a concrete, near-term checkpoint the guide (or a future Miner pass) could revisit in 2027 to see whether any of the 14 projects actually delivered public output — a useful marker for follow-up rather than a claim to act on now. Note that despite the five-country list, Singapore is named in the geographic-scope sentence but no Singapore-based grantee appears among the 14 named project summaries in the post — an inconsistency in the source itself, not an extraction error (see Extraction Notes).
