# Pushing software engineering limits with "napkin math"

> A practitioner interview with Simon Eskildsen (co-founder/CEO of turbopuffer, ex-Shopify infra
> engineer) on using memorized first-principles performance numbers ("napkin math") to challenge
> benchmark-driven design decisions, the durable-software lessons of an eight-year single-company
> tenure, how turbopuffer won Cursor as its first customer, and six reasons founders raise VC money
> (only one of which he considers healthy).

## Source Context

- **Type**: blog-post (The Pragmatic Engineer newsletter, Substack; free monthly issue, published
  2026-07-21). Written companion to a 55-minute on-stage interview Gergely Orosz conducted with Simon
  Eskildsen at the AI Engineer's World Fair. The article is not paywalled — full text is publicly
  accessible (no "for paid subscribers" gate encountered).
- **Author credibility**: Gergely Orosz is an ex-Uber engineering manager and author of The Pragmatic
  Engineer, previously documented in this corpus as a high-signal ~750k+ subscriber newsletter (see
  `survey-pragmaticengineer-ai-tooling-2026.md`). The interview subject, Simon Eskildsen, is a named,
  identifiable practitioner: an eight-year Shopify infrastructure engineer (creator of the open-source
  `toxiproxy` fault-injection tool) who co-founded turbopuffer, a vector-search infrastructure startup
  that reportedly crossed $100M ARR on under $1M of initial funding, with Cursor as its first customer.
  The piece discloses that turbopuffer is a season sponsor of Orosz's podcast, but states the article

## Extracted Claims

### Claim 1: "Napkin math" is a memorized, quantified first-principles toolkit (~50 numbers on latency, bandwidth, and cost) used to challenge benchmark-based design decisions rather than accept them at face value
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen describes maintaining a public GitHub table of infrastructure numbers, generated by a script, which he then memorized with flashcards so he could recall them instantly during design reviews.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single practitioner's self-described methodology; the GitHub table itself was referenced but not independently fetched in this extraction)
- **Quote**: "Napkin math was essentially just this table that I maintain on GitHub. There's probably like 50 of these numbers and then a script that generates them all."
- **Our assessment**: This is a specific, actionable practice — not just "think about first principles" but a concrete artifact (a maintained, scripted table) plus a memorization method (flashcards). The distinguishing feature versus generic "estimate before you build" advice is that Eskildsen treats the numbers as recallable facts, not something to look up per-decision, which is what let him challenge a benchmark claim in real time during a design review (see Claim 3).

### Claim 2: Several specific memorized figures: a gigabyte of DRAM costs about $2, a gigabyte of S3 storage costs about 2 cents, and DRAM bandwidth is roughly 100 GB/sec across multiple cores
- **Evidence**: Direct figures Eskildsen recites as examples of the numbers on his napkin-math table.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (self-reported figures, not benchmarked or dated to a specific cloud pricing snapshot in the article)
- **Quote**: "What does a gigabyte of memory cost? $2. What does a gigabyte of S3 cost? Two cents. What does a gigabyte of this cost? 10 cents."
- **Our assessment**: These are illustrative rather than exhaustive — the article explicitly says the full table (~50 numbers) lives on GitHub and is not reproduced in full here. Treat the specific dollar figures as directionally useful (order-of-magnitude reference points for compute vs. storage cost tradeoffs) rather than as current, precise cloud pricing.

### Claim 3: Napkin math is used to catch benchmark-driven design decisions that ignore an architectural detail — e.g., a "10-second" search benchmark result should have taken 10 milliseconds by the math, and the gap turned out to be an undisclosed distributed fan-out across 100 nodes
- **Evidence**: A worked example Eskildsen gives of a project team justifying "Database A over Database B" using a benchmark; he redid the math from DRAM bandwidth and data-volume assumptions and found the benchmark's real-world number was 1000x slower than the theoretical floor.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single recalled example, unnamed database/team/date)
- **Quote**: "You have DRAM bandwidth on multiple cores, at 100 GB/sec. So if you do the math, it should be 10 milliseconds. So, now you're telling me that the benchmark for the same thing takes 10 seconds, which means one of us is wrong!"
- **Our assessment**: The value here isn't the specific example but the diagnostic move: when reality and first-principles math disagree by an order of magnitude or more, treat the gap as a signal to investigate (his "first-principle gap" concept, see Claim 4) rather than accepting either number blindly. This is a concrete, teachable review heuristic distinct from generic "question your benchmarks" advice.

### Claim 4: When real-world performance beats the theoretical napkin-math floor (rather than falling short of it), that gap is worth investigating as a "first-principle gap" — it usually means the mental model is wrong, not that the system found free performance
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's account of investigating MySQL transactional throughput: napkin math predicted ~1,000 fsyncs/sec as a ceiling, but he measured ~5,300 insertions/sec — 5x higher than predicted. Investigating led him to discover MySQL's group-commit behavior (batching multiple fsyncs together), a technique he later reused for batching S3 writes at turbopuffer.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single named technical investigation, self-reported, but the batching mechanism it uncovered — group commit — is independently well-documented MySQL behavior)
- **Quote**: "Rarely is the system 5x faster than the napkin math says. When we identify a significant-looking gap between the real-life performance and the expected performance, I call it the 'first-principle gap.'"
- **Our assessment**: This is the most generalizable claim in the piece: napkin math isn't just a ceiling-setting exercise, it's a search for anomalies in either direction, and an unexplained anomaly is treated as incomplete understanding rather than a win to bank. The direct reuse of the group-commit insight (batch writes to reduce round-trip cost) as a turbopuffer design decision is a concrete instance of first-principles review directly informing production architecture.

### Claim 5: turbopuffer's founding motivation was a specific, quantified AI-era cost gap — a bootstrapped startup's vector-search vendor quote of $30K/month against an existing infra bill of only $5K/month, which made an AI feature economically unshippable
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen recounts helping friends at AI startups in early 2023 who faced this exact cost mismatch, driven by small (4-8KB) LLM context windows requiring fast retrieval to fill them.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single named cost example, unnamed startup and vendor)
- **Quote**: "One startup budgeted that their new search vendor would cost $30K/month, while the existing infra bill was only $5K/month!"
- **Our assessment**: This grounds turbopuffer's founding in a concrete, checkable-in-kind (if not checkable-in-fact) economic trigger rather than a purely technical curiosity — the company exists because a specific customer-facing feature was priced out of existence by vector-search infrastructure cost, which is a useful data point for any guide discussion of RAG/context-retrieval cost economics circa 2023.

### Claim 6: turbopuffer's first production version deliberately skipped a dedicated caching layer, storage-engine literature, and multi-node deployment — it ran S3-backed storage with an NGINX reverse-proxy cache on a single 8-core node, launched publicly via a tweet before scaling infrastructure
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's own account of the "simplest possible thing" architecture: cluster vectors into S3 files, search via centroid downloads, cache with NGINX in front of S3.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (self-reported architecture decision and its outcome)
- **Quote**: "For the first version, I didn't even implement a dedicated caching layer. I just put the reverse proxy (NGINX) in front of S3, and that was it! ... Why can't you work on a database like it's SaaS?"
- **Our assessment**: This is a concrete instance of "ship the simplest version and validate demand before hardening," applied to infrastructure software (a database), where Eskildsen explicitly notes that database engineers conventionally resist shipping unpolished work due to professional pride. Treat as a single case rather than a general rule for all infra software — the approach worked partly because turbopuffer's core invariant (no data loss on VM shutdown, since all writes went to blob storage) was already solid before the public launch tweet.

### Claim 7: turbopuffer won Cursor as its first customer by first building trust through unrelated, hands-on Postgres debugging (an autovacuum problem, diagnosed in person in Cursor's San Francisco office), before proposing its own unproven product
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's account of flying from Canada to San Francisco, diagnosing Cursor's existing Postgres performance issue with pganalyze, and only afterward discussing turbopuffer as a solution for Cursor's vector search.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single named customer-acquisition story, though independently corroborated in outline by a prior Pragmatic Engineer piece — see Cross-References)
- **Quote**: "When I showed up at Cursor's office, they were having some Postgres problems. ... I think this built enough trust with them to believe that if I know enough to help them with their database, maybe I also know how to build one."
- **Our assessment**: The sequencing is the interesting part — technical credibility was established on an unrelated problem before the actual product pitch. This is a distinct pattern from typical vendor sales narratives and worth citing as a concrete example of trust-building preceding a technology bet on an unproven, single-customer infra product.

### Claim 8: turbopuffer's optimization of Cursor's search reduced Cursor's indexing-and-search bill from roughly $80K/month to roughly $4K/month, a ~95% reduction, predicted in advance via napkin math and delivered
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen states he promised the 95% reduction based on first-principles calculation of Cursor's actual resource usage (without a margin for turbopuffer's own operating costs at the time), and that the promise was met.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single customer's before/after figures, self-reported by the vendor; no independent confirmation from Cursor in this article, though Cursor's side of the same story is referenced from a prior Pragmatic Engineer piece on Cursor's engineering, see Cross-References)
- **Quote**: "Cursor was spending around $80K/month on indexing and search; with turbopuffer in place, this dropped to $4K/month."
- **Our assessment**: A specific, large (95%) cost-reduction figure attributed to a named customer (Cursor) is a strong, quotable data point, but it comes from the vendor's own retelling rather than a Cursor-sourced figure in this article — treat the exact dollar amounts as anecdotal rather than audited.

### Claim 9: An eight-year single-company tenure taught Eskildsen that simple solutions built by one or two people in a week or two often outlast complex, multi-team, RFC-driven systems — this is presented as the single biggest benefit of long tenure
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's reflection on his time at Shopify, tied to specific examples: the `toxiproxy` fault-injection proxy he built (open-sourced 2014, reportedly still running in Shopify's CI 12 years later) and Shopify's sharding cutover completed a week before Black Friday.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single practitioner's retrospective judgment; the "still runs 12 years later" detail is attributed to Eskildsen's own knowledge, not independently verified against Shopify's current infrastructure)
- **Quote**: "Toxiproxy was open sourced in 2014, and apparently still runs in Shopify's CI system 12 years later, to Simon's knowledge! The biggest benefit of a long tenure at a company was learning to write software that ages well."
- **Our assessment**: This is a durability claim (simple > complex, over a 12-year horizon) grounded in one specific, nameable artifact (`toxiproxy`) rather than a vague generalization, which raises its evidentiary weight above a typical "keep it simple" platitude. Still a single case study, not a controlled comparison.

### Claim 10: Six legitimate reasons exist to raise VC funding — R&D, growth/marketing, employee liquidity, strategic partnerships, and M&A are treated as sound; "massaging founders' egos" is called out by name as the most common but most dangerous reason
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's own six-reason framework, developed after the interview as a reflection on turbopuffer's funding history (raised only $700K initially to hire two engineers, later allowed employee equity sales in a December 2025 round rather than a large ego-driven raise).
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (a single founder's post-hoc mental model, not a study or survey of VC-funded founders generally)
- **Quote**: "This is a very, very popular reason! You see big numbers, you get lots of press. But I think it's a really dangerous reason to raise money. I wish this reason was talked about more, because you are diluting all of your employees when you do it."
- **Our assessment**: This is a self-consistent framework (turbopuffer's own funding history — $700K seed, VCs who didn't take the ask seriously because it was "too little," a later employee-liquidity round rather than a status-driven mega-raise) rather than an abstract claim, which makes it more credible as lived practice than as general theory. Not directly about AI-native engineering, but relevant to the guide's coverage of founder/engineering-leader decision-making context, since VC-funding pressure is cited elsewhere in the corpus (see Cross-References) as a driver of engineering-process shortcuts.

### Claim 11: Simon Eskildsen's programming foundation was built through competitive algorithmic programming (Denmark's national team for the International Olympiad in Informatics) via self-directed study — a borrowed copy of "The Art of Computer Programming," USACO problems, and Wikipedia — rather than formal computer science education (he never attended college)
- **Evidence**: Eskildsen's own account of his teenage self-teaching process ahead of the IOI regional finals, and his subsequent direct-to-industry hire at Shopify (skipping college) after a viral personal blog post ("Why I'm glad my iPhone broke") caught a recruiter's attention.
- **Confidence**: anecdotal (single individual's biography)
- **Quote**: "I armed myself with a borrowed copy of 'The Art of Computer Programming', worked through the exercises, read up on common algorithms on Wikipedia, completed tasks on USACO, and memorized the critical parts of my Vim config for the competition computers."
- **Our assessment**: A biographical data point rather than a generalizable practice claim; relevant mainly as color establishing Eskildsen's credibility (deep, self-taught first-principles grounding) rather than as guide-actionable advice on its own. One transferable lesson he draws from the competition experience: "avoid digging holes" — abandon a fixated approach and restart from a blank page rather than patch edge-cases onto a broken general solution, on the reasoning that a correct general solution handles edge cases automatically.

```
  principles). A large gap almost never means the system beat the physics --
  it means the mental model is incomplete. Investigation found MySQL performs
  "group commit": batching multiple fsync operations together rather than
  syncing per-write. Eskildsen reused this batching insight for turbopuffer's
  S3 write path.
```

```
turbopuffer's v1 architecture, as described by Eskildsen (paraphrased/
summarized, not verbatim from source):
  - Store raw vector data in AWS S3
  - Run a clustering algorithm over vectors; write each cluster to its own
    file (cluster_01, cluster_02, ...)
  - Write a "centroids" file describing each cluster's center
  - Query path: download centroids, identify nearest cluster(s), download
    only those cluster files
  - Caching: NGINX reverse proxy in front of S3 -- no dedicated cache layer
    in v1
  - Deployment: single TMUX session on one 8-core GCP node at public launch
```

## Guide Impact

- **Chapter 00 (Principles)**: Add Claim 4 (the "first-principle gap" diagnostic: an anomaly between
  measured and theoretical performance is a signal of an incomplete mental model, not a free win) as a
  concrete decision-review heuristic. This complements any existing guidance on verifying AI-agent
  output against expectations — the same "don't just accept a surprising number, go find out why"
  discipline applies whether the surprising number comes from a vendor benchmark or an agent's
  self-reported test result.

- **Chapter 02 (Harness Engineering)**: Add Claim 6 (turbopuffer's deliberately unpolished, single-node
  v1, launched publicly to test demand before hardening) as a named case study for "ship the simplest
  version and validate before investing in robustness," specifically for infrastructure-adjacent
  software where engineers conventionally over-invest in polish before any user exists.
